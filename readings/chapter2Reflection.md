Reflection of chapter 2
Among the many iterations of Pong that Pippin Barr introduces in this chapter, the one that caught my attention the most was Ghost Pong. First of all, creating so many different iterations of Pong is not easy. It requires a huge number of ideas, and among all the possible variations I could imagine, I had never thought that making a game non-interactive could itself be an interesting possibility.
I was also inspired by Barr’s process of developing Ghost Pong. At first, he simply removed the collisions between the ball and the paddles, but he soon realized that this could be confusing for players because the paddles still looked solid and therefore appeared interactable. To solve this problem, he reduced the paddles’ opacity to 50%. This small visual change allows players to clearly see the ball passing through the paddles. The translucency makes this behavior feel much more visually and logically understandable, while also fitting perfectly with the name “Ghost Pong”.
This development process reminded me of how important visual design is in games. Visuals do not only shape a player’s impression of a game’s style; they can also communicate the game’s logic and directly affect how players understand its mechanics and interactions. Even a small visual adjustment, such as changing the opacity of an object, can completely change how players interpret what they are supposed to do—or, in the case of Ghost Pong, what they are not able to do.
This also reminded me of how often opacity is used in the games I play. For example, in The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom, when using the Recall ability, the path and movement of an object being rewound are represented through a transparent silhouette. Interestingly, this use of transparency works almost opposite to Ghost Pong. Instead of using transparency to suggest that something is not interactable, Tears of the Kingdom uses differences in opacity to guide players and show them what is going to happen next.
This visual effect is not only visually appealing but also gives players a sense of control. By seeing the transparent preview, players can anticipate the result of their actions before fully committing to them. If they are not satisfied with what is about to happen, they have a chance to reconsider their next move. In this way, opacity becomes more than an aesthetic choice—it becomes a form of visual communication between the game and the player.
